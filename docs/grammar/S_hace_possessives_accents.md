# Bundle S — Hace + time, possessives, accents (Lessons 64–67)

The bundle that finishes the past-point tense, hands you the **possessive words**
(*mi, tu, su, nuestro*), and demystifies **written accents** — they only mark stress, never
sound. Along the way you learn to say "ago" with *hace*, and you meet a recurring Spanish
habit: it leans on possessives far less than English does.

Four threads run through everything here:

```
[ hace + time ]   [ past-point endings ]   [ possessive ]   [ accent rule ]
  hace dos meses     -aste / -aron …          mi / su …        ¿dónde? vs donde
   ("two ago")      (you / they finished)    (the thing owned)  (stress, or look-alike)
```

> **Grammar terms** appear in *(italics)* next to the plain label, e.g. **owner word**
> *(possessive adjective)*. Plain label first so it reads like Mad Libs; the real term is
> there when you want it. **Slots** are written in `[brackets]` — fill them in.

---

## 1. Word groups

Words sorted by **the job they do in a sentence**, not by dictionary order. Drill the group, not the word.

### Owner words *(possessive adjectives)* — sit in front of the thing owned, like English "my / your"
| Spanish | English | note |
|---------|---------|------|
| `mi`      | my                  | no gender change; plural `mis` |
| `tu`      | your (informal)     | no gender change; plural `tus` (note: `tu`, no accent) |
| `su`      | his / her / their / your (formal) | one word covers all of them |
| `nuestro` | our                 | ends in `-o` → also flips for gender: `nuestra` |
| `propio`  | own                 | agrees too: `propia / propios / propias` |

They agree with the **thing owned**, not the owner. `su` only becomes `sus` when the
**thing** is plural — never because there are many "theys" (§2, possessive table).

### Past-point endings *(preterite endings)* — glue onto the verb stem to mean a finished action
| who | `-ar` verbs | `-er/-ir` verbs | example |
|-----|-------------|-----------------|---------|
| I             | `-é`    | `-í`    | hablé · comí |
| he/she/it/you-formal | `-ó`    | `-ió`   | habló · comió |
| you (informal)| `-aste` | `-iste` | hablaste · comiste |
| we            | `-amos` | `-imos` | hablamos · comimos |
| they / you-all| `-aron` | `-ieron`| hablaron · comieron |

`-aste`/`-iste` and `-aron`/`-ieron` are the two new ones this bundle. Mnemonic for
`comiste`: the **s** and **t** both whisper "you informal."

### Time-and-occasion words *(temporal nouns / adverbs)*
| Spanish | English | note |
|---------|---------|------|
| `hace`   | ago (lit. "it makes")        | from *hacer*; `hace dos semanas` = two weeks ago |
| `vez`    | time / occasion (f.)          | `una vez` once · `otra vez` again · `a la vez` at the same time |
| `hora`   | hour / clock time (f.)        | `¿a qué hora?` = at what time? (not *vez*) |
| `anoche` | last night                    | not "tonight"! |
| `esta noche` | tonight ("this night")    | |

### Body-and-pain words *(where Spanish drops the possessive)*
| Spanish | English | note |
|---------|---------|------|
| `doler` | to hurt        | o-splits: `duele` = it hurts |
| `me duele` | it hurts me | `me duele la pierna` = my leg hurts (no "my") |
| `dolor` | pain (noun)    | `dolor de cabeza` = headache |
| `pierna` / `cabeza` | leg / head | |

### Little words
| Spanish | English | term |
|---------|---------|------|
| `más`  | more / else / anymore | adds "else" to `nada`/`nadie`; with negation = "anymore" |
| `solo` | only / just | adverb; placed later = more emphatic |
| `de`   | of ("X's") | `la casa de José` = José's house |
| `propio` | own | (also in owner words above) |

## How "ago" works — the *hace* rule

Not a sentence structure, but it slots into almost every past-tense drill below.

```
English  "X time ago"   →   Spanish "it makes X time"   →   hace + [amount of time]
 two weeks ago               hace dos semanas                (stick it straight on)
 a month ago                 hace un mes                     (mes is masculine: un mes)
```

You attach the `hace` phrase right onto the sentence, **no extra words**:
`Lo vi hace dos semanas.` = I saw him two weeks ago.

## The /e/ ↔ /i/ flip — why `-er/-ir` verbs sound different in the past

```
PRESENT  -er/-ir is full of /e/   →   come, comen, comes, comemos   (one /i/ peeks in)
PAST-POINT it flips to full /i/   →   comí, comió, comiste, comimos  (one /e/ peeks in)
                                                                 └─ comieron
```

That lone /e/ in `-ieron` is a flag: it marks the `-er/-ir` group, the mirror image of the
lone /i/ that marks them in the present.

## 2. Sentence structures (drill these)

Each structure: a **shape** line (the only "code"), the slots you can swap, worked examples, then a **Drill** the student finishes out loud or on paper.

---

### S1 — "Did you ___?" — the you-form past-point *(2nd-person singular preterite)*
**shape:** `(¿)[verb-stem]+[-aste/-iste](?)`

- `-ar` verb → `-aste`: `hablaste` · `esperaste` · `llegaste` · `visitaste`
- `-er/-ir` verb → `-iste`: `comiste` · `saliste` · `viste`

```
¿Comiste?              Did you eat?
¿No comiste nada más?  Didn't you eat anything else?
¿Cuándo llegaste?      When did you arrive?
¿Me esperaste?         Did you wait for me?
```

A question is just the same sentence wrapped in `¿ … ?` — nothing reorders.

**Drill (EN → ES):** Did you arrive late? · Didn't you see me last night? · When did you arrive? · Did you wait for me?

---

### S2 — "They / you-all ___" — the they-form past-point *(3rd-person plural preterite)*
**shape:** `[verb-stem]+[-aron/-ieron]`

- `-ar` → `-aron`: `hablaron` · `esperaron` · `prepararon` · `llegaron`
- `-er/-ir` → `-ieron`: `comieron` · `salieron` · `vendieron` · `vieron`

```
Hablaron.                 They spoke.
Los vendieron.            They sold them.
Los vieron una vez.       They saw them once.
¿A qué hora llegaron?     At what time did they arrive?
```

**Drill:** They prepared it · They went out · They sold them · They arrived at your house

---

### S3 — "X time ago" — attaching *hace* *(temporal complement with hace)*
**shape:** `[past sentence] + hace + [amount of time]`

The `hace` phrase clips onto the end, no joining word.

| build | result | English |
|-------|--------|---------|
| Lo vi + hace + dos semanas | `Lo vi hace dos semanas.` | I saw him two weeks ago. |
| Le respondí + hace + siete semanas | `Le respondí hace siete semanas.` | I responded to her seven weeks ago. |
| Se lo respondí + hace + un mes | `Se lo respondí hace un mes.` | I responded it to her a month ago. |

**Drill:** I saw him two weeks ago · I responded to her a month ago · They arrived a week ago

---

### S4 — Spanish drops the possessive: pain & body parts *(dative of possession)*
**shape:** `me/te/le + duele + [el/la + body part]`

English says "my leg hurts"; Spanish says "it hurts **me** the leg." The `me/te/le`
carries the ownership, so no `mi`.

```
Me duele la pierna.         My leg hurts.        (lit. it-hurts-me the leg)
No me duele la pierna más.  My leg doesn't hurt anymore.   (más = "anymore" w/ negation)
Me duele la cabeza.         My head hurts.
Tengo dolor de cabeza.      I have a headache.   (noun route: dolor)
```

**Drill:** My leg hurts · My head hurts · My leg doesn't hurt anymore · I have a headache

---

### S5 — Owner words: "my / your / our ___" *(possessive adjectives)*
**shape:** `[possessive] + [noun]`

Possessives agree with the **thing owned**. Build with this table:

| possessive | singular thing | plural thing | gender flips? |
|------------|----------------|--------------|---------------|
| my         | `mi` amigo     | `mis` amigos | no |
| your (inf.)| `tu` casa      | `tus` casas  | no |
| his/her/their/your-formal | `su` casa | `sus` casas | no |
| our        | `nuestro` amigo / `nuestra` amiga | `nuestros` / `nuestras` | **yes** (`-o`) |
| own        | `mi propio` auto / `mi propia` casa | `mis propios ojos` | **yes** |

```
Mi casa es tu casa.            My house is your house.
Estoy con mis amigos.          I am with my friends.
Quiero ir a su casa.           I want to go to his house.   (one house)
Quiero ir a sus casas.         I want to go to his houses.  (sus = plural THING)
Lo vi con mis propios ojos.    I saw it with my own eyes.
```

`su` → `sus` **only** when the thing is plural — not because there are many owners.

**Drill:** my friends · our house · their house · their houses · my own car

---

### S6 — "X's house" — possession with *de* *(periphrastic genitive)*
**shape:** `el/la + [thing] + de + [owner]`

Spanish has no `'s`. It says "the house of X."

```
la casa de José              José's house
Vamos a la casa de José.     We're going to José's house.
Vamos a la fiesta de María.  We're going to Maria's party.
```

**Drill:** José's house · Maria's party · we're going to José's house

---

### S7 — Written accents: the decision list *(orthographic stress rules)*
Spanish accents only mark **which syllable is stressed** — they never change a vowel's
sound. Walk this list for any word:

1. **Ends in vowel, `n`, or `s`?** Stress falls on the **second-to-last** syllable by
   default. If that's where it really is → **no accent.** (`habla`, `hablan`, `hablamos`,
   `importante`, `diferente`)
2. **Ends in any other consonant?** Stress falls on the **last** syllable by default.
   If that's where it really is → **no accent.** (`comer`, `estar`, `hablar`, `salud`)
3. **Breaks the rule?** Add a written accent on the syllable actually stressed.
   (`tradición`, `opinión`, `limón` — end in `n`/vowel but stressed last; `hablé`, `habló`,
   `llegó`, `vivió` — end in vowel but stressed last)
4. **`estar` is the famous exception:** `estás`, `está`, `están` all carry an accent
   because they break rule #1.

Shortcut: glance at the present tense. Verbs end in vowel/`n`/`s` with second-to-last
stress and no accent — so the rule keeps regular verbs accent-free.

**Drill (where, if anywhere, does the accent go?):** tradicion · hablo (he spoke) · salud · importante · limon

---

### S8 — Accents that tell look-alike words apart *(diacritical accents)*
**shape:** one-syllable pairs, and question vs non-question words.

| accented | plain | how to tell |
|----------|-------|-------------|
| `sí` (yes)  | `si` (if)        | `Sí lo quiero` vs `Si lo quiero` |
| `tú` (you)  | `tu` (your)      | `Tú` vs `tu casa` |
| `mí` (me, after prep.) | `mi` (my) | `para mí`, `por mí` vs `mi casa` |
| `sé` (I know) | `se` (himself / "to her" from le→se) | `sé` vs `se lo respondí` |
| `dónde` (where?) | `donde` (where) | `¿Dónde estás?` vs "donde" in a statement |
| `cómo` (how?) | `como` (like / how) | question vs `como` |
| `cuándo` (when?) | `cuando` (when) | question vs `cuando` |
| `cuánto` (how much?) | `cuanto` (as much as) | question vs `cuanto` |

The question words wear the accent **even though they're not breaking the stress rule** —
it's purely to flag "this is a question word."

**Drill:** yes, I want it · if I want it · for me · my house · I know · where are you?

## 3. Same idea, said other ways

- **Point vs line in the past is often interchangeable.** `¿Dónde viviste?` (closed time,
  point) and `¿Dónde vivías?` (ongoing, line) can both work — the choice shows which idea
  is in the speaker's mind.
- **Headache, two ways:** `Tengo dolor de cabeza` (noun route) **or** `Me duele la cabeza`
  (verb route). Past-line: `Tenía dolor de cabeza` / `Me dolía la cabeza`.
- **"again" / "anymore":** `otra vez` = again; `más` after a negation = anymore
  (`no me duele más`).
- **"else":** add `más` to a negative word — `nada más` (nothing else), `nadie más`
  (nobody else). Double negatives are normal: `No comí nada más.`

---

## 4. Emphasis & bends

- **Spanish under-uses possessives.** Where English reaches for "my/your," Spanish often
  uses `me/te/le` (`me duele la pierna`) or `de` (`la casa de José`). Watch for it.
- **`solo` placement.** `solo hablé contigo` and `hablé contigo solo` both mean "I only
  spoke with you" — the later `solo` lands harder.
- **`le → se` before `lo/la`.** "I responded it to her" is `se lo respondí`, never *le lo*.
  (Same `se` that needs no accent in §S8, unlike `sé` = I know.)
- **Accents mark stress, not sound.** Unlike French, a Spanish accent never changes how the
  vowel is pronounced — it only points at the stressed syllable, or separates a look-alike.
